# Finance dictionary: first content review

Research date: 11 November 2026 (America/Phoenix). Status: draft for human review; not implemented or approved for publication.

## Recommendation and selection method

Start with the 42 entries below. The first 25 are general investing essentials; the next 5 support the early bond lessons; the final 12 support company investigation. These are editorial priorities, not a statistically measured ranking of the most searched words.

Selection criteria: appears in authoritative introductory investing material; helps explain several other concepts; supports an early OPS learning task; or prevents a consequential beginner misunderstanding. The curriculum emphasizes investments and company analysis, so this is not intended as a complete personal-finance dictionary.

The selection follows the topics in [Investor.gov's introduction to investing](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing), its [allocation and diversification guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation), and the [SEC's introductory financial-statement guide](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/beginners-guide-financial-statements). FINRA supplies additional bond and volatility explanations; the Federal Reserve supplies the inflation definition.

Every professional-source summary below is a paraphrase, not a quotation. The proposed simple wording is an original OPS adaptation. The linked pages contain the institutions' own explanations. US regulatory descriptions should be labeled as US-specific when used; the conceptual definitions usually travel more broadly.

## Original OPS examples for reviewing the reading level

These are invented illustrations, not forecasts, source quotations, or recommended investments. Dollar examples ignore fees and taxes unless stated otherwise.

1. **Compound interest:** Start with $100 at a fixed 5% yearly interest rate, compounded annually. After one year you have $105. In year two, interest is $5.25, bringing the balance to $110.25. The extra $0.25 comes from interest on the first year's interest. A stock investment does not promise this fixed return.
2. **Inflation:** A representative shopping basket costs $100, then $103 a year later. That is a 3% price increase for the basket. The same $100 now buys less of it. One product's price increase alone does not establish economy-wide inflation.
3. **Total return:** Buy a share for $100. It pays a $3 dividend, which you keep as cash, and you sell it for $105. Your gain is $8, or 8%, before fees and taxes. Counting the price change alone would miss the dividend.
4. **Allocation and rebalancing:** A hypothetical portfolio has $700 in stocks and $300 in bonds: 70% and 30%. If it later holds $900 in stocks and $300 in bonds, the mix is 75% and 25%. With total value unchanged at $1,200 during the trade, moving $60 from stocks to bonds restores $840 and $360, or 70% and 30%. This is arithmetic, not a suggested portfolio.
5. **Expense ratio:** At an illustrative constant average investment value of $2,000, a 0.25% annual expense ratio represents about $5 of yearly fund expenses. Actual dollars depend on investment values over time. Other charges may also apply.
6. **Coupon and current yield:** A conventional fixed-rate bond has $1,000 face value and a 4% annual coupon rate: $40 interest per year. If it pays twice yearly, each payment is $20. At a market price of $800, its current yield is $40 / $800 = 5%. The 4% coupon rate has not changed. Current yield omits the eventual gain or loss on principal and is not a promised total return.
7. **Market capitalization:** An invented company has 2 million outstanding shares priced at $15 each. Its market capitalization is $30 million. This calculation prices the shares; it does not establish what someone would pay to acquire the whole business including its debt.
8. **EPS and P/E:** In a deliberately simple company with no preferred shares, no potential dilution, and 1 million common shares throughout the year, $2 million of profit means EPS of $2. At a $24 share price, trailing P/E is 12. EPS is an accounting measure, not an automatic payment to each shareholder.

## Editorial decisions requiring care

- **ETF and index fund:** Keep separate entries. ETF describes a fund structure; following an index describes a strategy. The SEC describes both active and index ETFs. A fund can concentrate its risks. [SEC ETF explanation](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-2).
- **Risk and volatility:** Preserve separate entries. Price fluctuation is one aspect of investment risk; definitions must also accommodate business failure and inability to sell. [SEC risk explanation](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/what-risk).
- **Coupon conventions:** The SEC glossary's coupon-rate entry describes a semiannual rate. FINRA's worked bond explanation clearly separates the annual rate from twice-yearly payments. Use the latter convention explicitly for the proposed example; do not copy the glossary sentence without qualification. [SEC coupon-rate entry](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary), [FINRA bond explanation](https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investment-products/bonds).
- **Yield:** Use the precise name “current yield” in this starter set. The SEC current-yield prose refers to an interest rate divided by a price, while its example uses annual interest dollars. FINRA explicitly uses annual coupon dollars. Follow the dimensionally correct dollar formula. Yield to maturity needs a separate entry and explanation later. [SEC current yield](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/current-yield), [FINRA yield explanation](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/bond-yield-return).
- **Company definitions:** Revenue can precede cash collection. Accounting equity differs from market capitalization. These distinctions need examples in the finished entries. [SEC statements guide](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/beginners-guide-financial-statements).
- **EPS and P/E:** The SEC glossary gives introductory versions. Before building advanced examples, source the weighted-average share count, preferred-dividend treatment, and diluted EPS separately. Label trailing versus forecast earnings; do not imply a low P/E proves a bargain. [SEC EPS](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/earnings-share), [SEC P/E](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/price-earnings-pe-ratio).
- **Source quality:** Institutional authority is a starting point, not a substitute for checking the precise definition and arithmetic. The reviewed FINRA yield page also uses a simple annual average in its multi-year example. That should not become a definition of compound annual return.
