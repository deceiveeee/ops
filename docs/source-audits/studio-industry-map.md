# The industry map

What the map surface may say, and the sentence in the paper that lets it say so.

Same edition as the other two Moat audits: Mauboussin and Callahan, *Measuring the Moat*,
Consilient Observer, **15 October 2024**, read from
[`docs/source-pdfs/measuring-the-moat-2024-10-15.pdf`](../source-pdfs/measuring-the-moat-2024-10-15.pdf).
This audit covers **pp. 13-14** and the checklist's Lay of the Land section, **p. 67**.

## What the paper says a map is for

> Constructing an industry map is a good place to start the analysis of an industry... The goal is
> to include all of the companies or entities that may have an impact on the profitability of the
> firm you are analyzing. (p. 13)

And how it is laid out:

> Suppliers, which provide the firm with its inputs, are typically on the left side of the map.
> Customers, the purchasers of goods or services, are on the right. We also consider factors such
> as government regulations, tariffs, and labor markets. (p. 13)

Exhibit 9, the map of the U.S. airline industry, puts government across the top and a box of
"Other factors (affect all)" at the bottom — economic conditions, geopolitical risk, climate
change, global pandemic. Studio's zones are those four plus the focal company, and no others.

Three further instructions, each of which the surface carries:

- **Size on the page.** "We recommend listing firms in each section in terms of market share so
  that relative size is immediately evident." (p. 13)
- **Who might arrive.** "it is useful to include potential new entrants to the degree that they can
  be identified or contemplated." (p. 13)
- **What kind of relationship it is.** "It is also important to understand the nature of the
  economic interactions between the organizations on the map." (p. 13)

## The relationship kinds

The paper's own list, p. 13, with its own examples. These are the options the surface offers, and
the examples are shown beside them:

| Kind | The paper's example |
| --- | --- |
| Non-contractual | travellers buying airline tickets |
| Contractual | software-as-a-service |
| Cost-plus contract | government acquiring defense systems |
| Best-efforts contract | an investment bank underwriting a security for an issuer |
| Licence | a movie based on a doll |
| Option | an airline purchasing aircraft |
| Some other arrangement | the paper's own last item |

The paper adds one thing to look for alongside them: "Identifying potential agency costs is also
useful. These costs arise when an agent, a party acting on behalf of another firm that is the
principal, takes actions that harm the principal." (p. 13) That definition is glossed in place
where the surface mentions it.

## Why the connections are worth the work

> Research shows investors may profit from understanding the connections that industry maps
> identify. Specifically, when shocks to one firm in the system affect other companies, because of
> either supply or demand links, the market often fails to reflect the information on a timely
> basis... Further, sell-side equity analysts who follow suppliers and customers produce earnings
> forecasts that are more accurate on average than analysts who follow only suppliers. (p. 13)

The surface says this once, as the reason to bother, and does not turn it into a promise: the
paper says investors *may* profit, and reports a finding about analysts' accuracy, not a method.

## The worked example

Exhibit 9 is the paper's own map of the U.S. airline industry, and Studio shows it as the model
before asking a learner to build one. It is reproduced as the paper lists it, in its zones, with
its own note that LCC means low-cost carrier. The source line says Counterpoint Global, because
that is what the exhibit says.

The airlines themselves carry the paper's tags — hub, LCC, regional — and those are the paper's
words, not Studio's reading of those businesses.

## What a learner does

They build the map for the company they are investigating: name an entity, put it in one of the
paper's zones, say what kind of relationship it is where that applies, say in their own words how
it affects the company's profits, and attach a passage they kept from the company's own filings.

Two parts of the map are already in the project and are drawn without being asked for again:

- **The focal company** is the investigation itself.
- **Inputs the learner has already linked** in the reader — each is something they decided the
  company buys, evidenced by a passage — are drawn on the supplier side, and say they came from
  there.
- **Competitors they have already added** are drawn beside the focal company, because Exhibit 9
  lists the airlines together in the middle.

## What is deliberately not built

- **No market shares on a learner's own map.** The paper recommends ordering each section by share
  so relative size shows, and Studio has share figures for five SIC codes and none for an arbitrary
  supplier or customer. Inventing an order would be inventing the fact the order is meant to carry.
  The surface says so where the paper's recommendation appears, and the industry surface, which
  does have measured shares, is linked from it.
- **No profit pool on the map.** It is the next section of the paper (p. 15). Drawn across a map —
  Exhibit 10's pool by activity, suppliers and customers beside the industry — it needs ROIC, WACC
  and invested capital for every kind of participant, and Studio has none of that for a supplier a
  learner has just named. Drawn within one industry by company, as Exhibit 11 draws the airlines, it
  needs only the industry's own companies, and Studio has those for its five researched industries:
  that form is built on its own page, `/studio/pool`, audited in
  [`studio-profit-pool.md`](studio-profit-pool.md), and linked from this surface's source panel.
  (This entry said until 2026-09-23 that no pool could be built at all. That was true of Exhibit 10
  only.)
- **No suggested entities.** Studio does not know who supplies or buys from an arbitrary company,
  and a list of plausible-looking names would be a guess wearing the clothes of research. What it
  can do — and does — is point at the company's own filings, where the names actually are.
- **Nothing inferred from an SEC registrant list.** The workspace proposal is explicit that a
  registrant sample is not the economic market, and a map built from one would quietly drop every
  private and foreign participant.

## Terms defined in place

*Supplier*, *customer*, *intermediary*, *agency cost*, *cost-plus*, *best-efforts*, *licence*,
*option*, *low-cost carrier*. The framework's own name — the industry map — is the plain phrase
already, so it is used as the paper uses it.
