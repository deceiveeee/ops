What the competition surface may say, and the sentence in the paper that lets it say so.

## The five forces, as Exhibit 17 sets them out

Verbatim. The paper orders them this way and the surface keeps that order.

| Force | Risk | Mitigant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Threat of New Entrants | New competitors erode market share and profitability. | Establish strong barriers to entry. |
| Rivalry Among Existing Firms | Intense competition reduces profitability. | Avoid direct competition and promote legal cooperation. |
| Bargaining Power of Suppliers | Strong suppliers increase input costs and squeeze margins. | Diversify supplier base and consider vertical integration. |
| Bargaining Power of Buyers | Strong buyers are demanding and limit profitability. | Differentiate and promote customer loyalty. |
| Threat of Substitutes | Substitutes offer products that limit industry profitability. | Innovate, improve products, and create switching costs. |

The paper says plainly that two of the five carry more weight: "While all of the forces are
important, we believe that threat of new entrants and rivalry among existing firms deserve more
extensive consideration than the others" (p. 22). It gives the other three a paragraph each and
gives entrants ten pages. Greenwald is quoted calling entrants "the one force that dominates all
the others" (p. 23). The surface reflects that weighting by ordering entrants and rivalry first,
and by saying so; it does not score or weight the forces numerically, which the paper never does.

## The five framing points, which decide the whole design

These are the paper's own cautions before the analysis starts (p. 22), and each one rules
something out of the interface:

1. **"The goal is not to determine whether an industry is attractive but rather to understand the
   drivers of profitability. Specifically, you can assess each force in the context of what it
   means for prices and costs. This analysis includes consideration of the income statement and
   the balance sheet."** → the surface never produces a verdict on an industry. Every finding a
   learner records must name what it does to **prices, costs, the capital the business needs, or
   the opportunities it can invest in**. Those four are this sentence, with "the balance sheet"
   read as capital and opportunities.
2. **"Strategy is not about a company beating its competitors. It is about figuring out how to
   earn a sustainable economic profit."** → the surface never ranks a company against rivals as
   the point of the exercise.
3. **"You want to separate what is structural from what is temporary or cyclical."** → every
   finding is asked which it is, and neither answer is the better one.
4. **"The structure by no means seals the fate of the companies in the industry."** → no force,
   and no combination of them, may produce a conclusion about the company.
5. **"Much of what is put forth as analysis of industry structure is simply listing pluses and
   minuses for each of the forces. The objective is to go beyond the superficial to get a complete
   view of the drivers of profit."** → **this rules out five boxes with a high/medium/low picker.**
   A finding is not accepted as one until it names a mechanism, says where it bites, and records
   what would change it.

Point 5 is the same instruction the workspace proposal gives in its own words — "avoid five empty
boxes asking a beginner to invent a Five Forces analysis" — arrived at independently.

## The determinant questions

Every question offered under a force is from the paper. Those marked *checklist* are verbatim from
pp. 67-68; those marked *p. 23* and *pp. 27, 31-32* are put as questions from the paper's prose,
and the prose is quoted beside them in the interface.

### Bargaining power of suppliers

- How much leverage do suppliers have? *(checklist)*
- Can companies pass on price increases from their suppliers? *(checklist)*
- Are suppliers more concentrated than the industry they serve? *(p. 23)*
- Would switching to another supplier be costly? *(p. 23)*
- Is what they supply a small part of total input cost, or critical to the buyer? *(p. 23)*

Prose relied on, p. 23: "Suppliers are well positioned if they are more concentrated than the
industry they serve, if substitute products do not burden them, or if their offerings have high
switching costs. They are also well situated if they represent a relatively small percentage of
the total input costs of the buyers they sell to or if the product or service is critical to the
buyer."

### Bargaining power of buyers

- How much leverage do buyers have? *(checklist)*
- How informed are the buyers? *(checklist)*
- Are the buyers concentrated, or spread thin? *(p. 23)*
- Would it cost a buyer anything to go elsewhere? *(p. 23)*
- How much does this purchase matter to the buyer? *(p. 23)*

Prose relied on, p. 23: "It is a function of buyer concentration, switching costs, levels of
information, substitute products, and the importance of the offering to the buyer. Large buyers
that are informed have much more leverage over their suppliers than do diffused buyers that are
uninformed."

### Threat of substitutes

- Are there substitute products? *(checklist)*
- Can you identify the source and size of switching costs? *(checklist)*
- Would a customer actually switch to one? *(p. 23)*

Prose relied on, p. 23: "A business faces a substitution threat if its prices are not competitive
and if comparable products are available from other companies. Substitute products limit the
prices that companies can charge and place a ceiling on potential returns."

### Threat of new entrants

Taken from the checklist (p. 67) and from Porter's seven barriers (p. 27): supply-side economies
of scale, capital requirements, demand-side benefits of scale, customer switching costs,
incumbency advantages independent of size, unequal access to distribution channels, and
restrictive government policy.

- What is the history of entry and exit in the industry? *(checklist — the paper recommends
  "examining the history of entry and exit early in the process of analyzing an industry", p. 23)*
- What is the level of minimum efficient scale? *(checklist)*
- How much capital would a new competitor have to put up? *(p. 27)*
- Are there network effects, and if so, how strong are they? *(checklist)*
- Would a customer have to pay something to switch to an entrant? *(p. 27)*
- Do incumbents have valuable licenses or patents? *(checklist)*
- Have incumbents benefitted from the learning curve? *(checklist)*
- Can an entrant reach customers, or do incumbents hold the channels? *(p. 27)*
- Have incumbents entrenched themselves by shaping regulation? *(checklist)*
- Are incumbents known to be aggressive in deterring entry? *(checklist)*
- How specific are the assets in the industry? *(checklist)*

### Rivalry among existing firms

- Is the industry growing? *(checklist)*
- Are there many competitors of roughly equal size? *(p. 31)*
- Is there tacit coordination for pricing and capacity addition decisions? *(checklist)*
- How variable is industry demand? *(checklist)*
- Are fixed costs high? *(checklist)*
- How similar are the firms in terms of incentives, time horizon, and ownership structure? *(checklist)*
- Is it costly to leave this industry? *(p. 32)*

Prose relied on, p. 31: "Rivalry tends to be more intense when there are lots of competitors and
they are of equal size... The likelihood of coordination rises when the industry has a few large
companies that can monitor and signal to one another." And p. 32: "Competitors are more likely to
stick it out and try to compete if it is costly to leave an industry."

The weapons of rivalry, p. 31, quoted in the interface: "pricing, service offerings, capacity
changes, new products, advertising, and promotional spending."

## The worked example

Porter's airline analysis, as the paper reports it. This is the model a learner sees before being
asked to do anything, and it is labelled as Porter's, on airlines, not as Studio's.

| Force | Verdict | The paper's reasons, p. 23 and p. 25 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | High | "many airline employees are members of labor unions, airframe and aircraft engine manufacturers are oligopolies, and airports operate as local monopolies" |
| Bargaining power of buyers | High | buyers "are largely fragmented, have low switching costs (offset to some degree by frequent flier programs), have the means to compare fares easily, and are price sensitive as they perceive air travel to be a relatively standard offering" |
| Threat of substitutes | Medium | "Travel by train or car can substitute for short-haul flights, and business meetings via videoconferencing can replace some face-to-face gatherings" |
| Threat of new entrants | High | "reflecting limited advantages for incumbents, low switching costs, and easy access to distribution channels", against barriers of "sizeable capital requirements to acquire aircraft, regulatory costs, and limited access to airport gates and takeoff and landing slots" |

The paper gives no rivalry verdict for airlines, so the worked example says so rather than
inventing one.

## What is deliberately not built

- **No score, grade or total.** The paper produces none, and its fourth framing point says
  structure does not seal a company's fate. A high count of forces is not a finding.
- **No high/medium/low picker per force for the learner's own company.** Porter's airline
  verdicts are shown as his; asking a beginner for the same judgement in one click is the
  "listing pluses and minuses" the paper's fifth point rejects.
- **No industry-wide data behind the forces.** The industry surface has entry counts and share
  instability for five SIC codes; the forces are a company's own reading of its own industry, and
  wiring the two together would imply a coverage Studio does not have. The entry-and-exit question
  links to the industry surface where that industry is one of the five.
- **Nothing from the paper's later sections.** Barriers, disruption, the value chain, the value
  stick, government, firm interaction and brands each get their own surface and their own audit.
  This audit covers pp. 22-32 and the checklist's three competition sections only.

## Terms defined in place

A learner meets these here for the first time, so the interface defines each where it is used, in
the paper's sense: *bargaining power*, *substitute*, *switching cost*, *barrier to entry*,
*minimum efficient scale*, *network effect*, *economies of scale*, *tacit coordination*,
*operating leverage*, *asset specificity*. The source's own label for the framework — five forces
— is kept and glossed on first use, as a cited framework's labels must be.
